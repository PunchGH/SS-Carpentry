# TV Feature Walls & Architectural Millwork — Smartphone Shot List

**For:** SS Carpentry on-site photography  
**Goal:** Capture 6–8 quick, high-impact photos of every completed TV wall on your phone before packing up tools.  
**Estimated Time on Site:** 7–10 minutes per project.

---

## Why These Photos Matter

Custom TV walls and architectural millwork are the most photogenic, high-value work you build. Discerning homeowners in Westboro, The Glebe, and Rockcliffe decide whether to commission a $5,000–$10,000 feature wall within three seconds of seeing your work. 

You do **not** need a professional photographer or a DSLR camera. Modern smartphone cameras (iPhone, Samsung, Pixel) produce stunning results if you follow these specific angles and light setups.

---

## The #1 Most Important Shot: The On/Off Lighting Pair

> **This 2-photo pair is the single most persuasive sales tool on your entire website.**  
> It proves the dramatic transformation your concealed LED channel lighting creates at night.

### How to shoot the on/off pair:
1. **Position yourself:** Stand 10–12 feet directly in front of the TV wall, dead-center. Hold your phone at chest height (around 4.5 feet high). Lean your elbows against a wall, chair, or doorframe to keep the camera completely steady.
2. **Shot A — Daylight / Ambient:**
   - Room lights ON (or normal afternoon daylight).
   - LED backlight OFF.
   - TV turned off (or displaying a clean art screen/wallpaper).
   - Snap the photo.
3. **Shot B — Evening 2700K Warm Glow:**
   - **DO NOT MOVE YOUR FEET OR YOUR PHONE.** Keep the exact same camera framing.
   - Turn OFF the main room ceiling lights.
   - Turn ON the concealed LED backlighting to 100%.
   - Tap the screen on the illuminated wood edge to set focus.
   - Snap the photo.

*When uploaded to the website, visitors can slide or toggle between these two shots.*

## The Complete 8-Shot Checklist

Before sweeping up and handing over the keys, take these 8 shots:

### 1. The Straight-On Hero (Wide Context)
- **What to show:** The entire wall from floor to ceiling, including baseboards and ceiling crown/shadow reveal.
- **Angle:** Dead-center, standing straight on.
- **Why it matters:** Proves the scale, proportion, and how cleanly the feature wall integrates into the room architecture.

### 2. The Night Atmosphere (LED Backlight On)
- **What to show:** The exact same frame as Shot 1, with room lights off and your concealed 2700K warm LED channels shining.
- **Angle:** Dead-center.

### 3. Zero-Wire Proof (Cable Management Detail)
- **What to show:** A close view looking underneath the TV and behind the floating console.
- **Angle:** Low 45-degree angle pointing slightly upward.
- **Why it matters:** Homeowners hate dangling black cords. This shot proves that 100% of HDMI cables, power cords, and low-voltage wires are completely hidden in in-wall conduits.

### 4. TV Mounting & Slat Alignment Profile
- **What to show:** Side view of the TV sitting against the wall.
- **Angle:** Side profile (standing 3–4 feet away at a 30-degree angle to the wall).
- **Why it matters:** Shows whether the screen is ultra-flush against the acoustic slats or recessed into a custom niche, with no ugly brackets visible.

### 5. Joinery & Mitres Close-Up (Craftsmanship)
- **What to show:** Sharp, close-up details of 45-degree corner mitres on the floating console, clean edge scribing against uneven walls/ceilings, and the even spacing of acoustic slats over felt.
- **Distance:** 12 to 18 inches away from the wood.
- **Why it matters:** Handymen can buy slatted panels; only a true carpenter cuts seamless mitres and scribes tight to Ottawa century home walls.

### 6. Floating Console & Soundbar Integration
- **What to show:** The lower wall-hung credenza, revealing clean shadow-line reveals, soft-close drawers, or custom-fit soundbar niches.
- **Angle:** Mid-distance (5–6 feet away), showing the floating effect and floor clearance.

### 7. The In-Progress Framing Shot ("Proof We Built It")
- **What to show:** A quick snap during the rough-in stage before panels go up. Capture the structural 2x6 timber backing, in-wall cable conduit chases, and electrical rough-in boxes.
- **Why it matters:** Proves you build real, structural, heavy-load blocking that can carry 100"+ displays without tearing out of the drywall.

### 8. The "Before" Snapshot
- **What to show:** The blank, boring drywall with a dangling power cord before you started demolition or framing.
- **Angle:** Same position where the finished hero shot will be taken.

## 5 Quick Phone Camera Tips (2 Minutes to Read)

1. **Wipe your lens on your shirt:**  
   Pocket lint and thumb grease make bright LED lights look like blurry, smudged starbursts. One quick wipe makes LED lines crisp and razor-sharp.
2. **Use 1.5x or 2x zoom (stand further back):**  
   Avoid the 0.5x ultra-wide lens. Ultra-wide bends straight vertical slats into curved fish-eye lines. Stand back 10–14 feet and use standard 1x or 2x optical zoom to keep all lines perfectly straight and vertical.
3. **Turn on Camera Grid Lines:**  
   In your phone settings (`Settings` &rarr; `Camera` &rarr; `Grid` on iPhone/Android), turn Grid ON. Line up the vertical grid lines with your wood slats or wall corners so your photo is never tilted.
4. **Tap to Focus & Pull Down Exposure:**  
   When shooting the LED backlight shot, tap the screen on the wooden slat edge. If the lights look too bright or blown out, slide your finger down slightly on the sun/brightness slider. The wood grain should remain visible in the warm glow.
5. **Clear the Stage:**  
   Move toolboxes, vacuums, coffee cups, and tape rolls out of the frame. Ensure the floating console surface has nothing on it or only 1–2 clean decor pieces (a plant, a hardcover book).
